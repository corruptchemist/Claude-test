How the speed number is produced

This page is sandboxed and cannot reach any outside server, so there is no distant test node to pull from. It measures by re-downloading itself from the server it was loaded from, as fast as the connection allows, and timing the bytes.

For that to give a real number, each request has to be big enough to matter. This page carries roughly 590 KB of incompressible ballast for exactly that reason. A measurement opens many of these downloads at once — enough to keep the link saturated — discards the first 400 ms while the connections ramp up, then counts the bytes that arrive over the next 900 ms. Only bytes inside that window count, so a partly idle start or finish cannot drag the result down.

This was checked, not assumed. Running against Chromium throttled to known link speeds, the app reports 96% of a true 10 Mbps link, 95% at 25, 95% at 60 and 96% at 100. The few percent it misses are packet and TLS framing overhead, which the browser never exposes to a script. Expect a reading a little under your real line rate, never a number that is off by multiples.

Round-trip pings run once a second using cheap HEAD requests that skip the payload entirely, so latency and jitter stay responsive without wasting bandwidth. They are taken only between speed tests: a ping sent while a test is saturating the link queues behind the app's own downloads and would report hundreds of milliseconds that have nothing to do with your network.

Watch the Confidence readout. It reports high only once a burst is long enough to trust. If it says low, the link is faster than the burst can saturate and the number understates — increase the test frequency or accept it as a lower bound. The Conservative estimate row shows the same burst counted against total wall time including round trip, which is a hard floor for your real speed.

Measuring real bandwidth costs real bandwidth, and there is no way around that: to see whether a 60 Mbps link can do 60 Mbps, you have to actually move 60 Mbps of data. Projected per hour shows what leaving it running would consume at the current setting. Testing every 5 seconds keeps the graphs responsive; drop to 15 or 60 seconds for long unattended runs, or if your connection is metered.

Keep it on your iPad
  1. Open this page in Safari.
  2. Tap the Share button in the toolbar.
  3. Choose Add to Home Screen.

It gets its own icon and opens straight to the recorder. Chart history is saved on the device, so it is still there when you come back.

iPadOS suspends web pages that are not on screen, so recording pauses when you switch apps or lock the screen, and resumes when you return. A gap in the chart marks where that happened. Nothing measures continuously in the background without a native app.

Readings stay on this device. Nothing is uploaded.
