Continuity, End Behavior & IVT

Three topics, three questions. Continuity asks whether the graph has a break at a specific spot. End behavior asks where the graph goes at the far left and far right. IVT uses continuity to prove a zero is hiding somewhere, without ever finding it. Everything below is built on the exact problems from your worksheet.

Lesson 1

What continuity actually means

The everyday version: a function is continuous on an interval if you can draw its graph there without lifting your pencil. Every break in the pencil line is a discontinuity.

That picture is right, but it won't get you through the worksheet, because you're usually handed a formula and no graph. So there's a checklist version. To test continuity at one specific input x = c, ask three questions in order:

The three-part continuity test at x = c

  1. Does the function have a value there? Is f(c) defined — i.e. is c in the domain?
  2. Does the graph agree from both sides? Walk toward c from the left and from the right. Do both approaches head for the same height? That common height is the limit, limx→c f(x).
  3. Do those two answers match? Is limx→c f(x) = f(c)?

All three yes → continuous at c. Any one fails → discontinuous at c, and which one fails tells you what kind of break it is.

The notation for "from the left" and "from the right" is a little minus or plus on the arrow:

  • limx→c− f(x) — the left-hand limit: what the outputs approach as x creeps up to c from smaller numbers.
  • limx→c+ f(x) — the right-hand limit: same thing coming down from larger numbers.

The two-sided limit exists only when those two agree. That single sentence is the engine behind every piecewise problem on your sheet.

Where can a break even happen?

You don't check every number — you check suspects. In precalculus there are only three kinds:

SuspectWhere it comes fromExample from your sheet
A denominator equal to zeroSet the bottom = 0 and solve#4: 4x − 16 = 0 at x = 4
The split point of a piecewise functionThe x-value where the rule changes#2: the rule changes at x = −2
The edge of a radical or logWhere the inside stops being allowedp.22 #4: √−b − x ends at x = −3

Everything else is automatically continuous. Polynomials have no denominators and no pieces, so they are continuous at every real number, full stop. That is the entire content of problem #1 — f(x) = x³ − 4x² + 3 is a polynomial, so it is continuous at x = 4 and everywhere else. You may write "polynomial, therefore continuous" as your reason.

Testing a piecewise function

Here is the whole move: plug the split point into each rule separately. The rule for the left side gives you the left-hand limit; the rule for the right side gives you the right-hand limit. Then compare.

Worked — page 17 #2

f(x) = x² + 8x + 15 when x < −2, and f(x) = −2x − 10 when x ≥ −2. Test x = −2.

From the left (use the top rule, since that one owns x < −2):
(−2)² + 8(−2) + 15 = 4 − 16 + 15 = 3

From the right (bottom rule, which owns x ≥ −2):
−2(−2) − 10 = 4 − 10 = −6

Compare: 3 ≠ −6. The sides disagree, so the two-sided limit doesn't exist — test step 2 fails. The graph leaps from height 3 to height −6. That's a jump discontinuity at x = −2.

Worked — page 17 #3

f(x) = −x − 1 when x ≤ −4, and f(x) = x + 7 when x > −4. Test x = −4.

Left: −(−4) − 1 = 3. Right: −4 + 7 = 3. They agree, so the limit is 3.
And f(−4) itself? The ≤ sign means the first rule owns x = −4, so f(−4) = 3 too.
All three conditions pass → continuous at x = −4. The two pieces meet at a single point on the graph.

The mistake everyone makes

Plugging the split point into only the rule whose inequality contains it. That gives you f(c) — step 1 — and tells you nothing about whether there's a break. You must evaluate both rules at c, even though one of them technically isn't allowed to be used there. That's the point: you're asking where that piece was heading.

Lesson 2

Naming the break: the three kinds

Once you know a discontinuity is there, the worksheet wants you to classify it. There are exactly three names, and they look like this:

NameWhat failsHow to spot it in the algebra
Removable
(a "hole")		The limit exists — both sides head to the same height — but the function has no value there, so step 1 or 3 failsA factor cancels completely between numerator and denominator
JumpLeft and right limits are different numbers, so step 2 failsAlmost always a piecewise function whose two rules disagree at the split
Infinite
(vertical asymptote)		The outputs blow up to ±∞, so no limit existsA factor in the denominator that doesn't cancel

The rational-function routine

For anything of the form (polynomial)/(polynomial), one procedure answers every question on pages 17–21:

Factor first. Always.

  1. Factor the numerator and the denominator completely.
  2. Find every x that makes the original denominator zero. These, and only these, are your discontinuities.
  3. Cancel any matching factors.
  4. For each bad x: did its factor cancel? Cancelled → hole (removable). Still downstairs → vertical asymptote (infinite).
  5. Zeros of the function come from what's left upstairs, set equal to zero.
Worked — page 17 #6

f(x) = x − 5x² − 8x + 15, asked at x = 3 and x = 5.

Factor: x − 5(x − 3)(x − 5). Bad x-values: 3 and 5.

Cancel: the (x − 5) pair goes away, leaving 1x − 3.

Classify: x = 5 cancelled → removable discontinuity (a hole at height 1/(5−3) = ½). x = 3 survived in the denominator → infinite discontinuity, vertical asymptote x = 3.

Don't lose the hole

After cancelling, 1/(x − 3) looks perfectly happy at x = 5 — but the original function was never defined there, and cancelling does not bring it back to life. x = 5 stays out of the domain forever. Write down your bad x-values before you cancel, or you will lose one every time.

Check yourself: classify the discontinuities of f(x) = −x + 4x² + 9x + 20  (#7)

Denominator factors as (x + 4)(x + 5), so the suspects are x = −4 and x = −5. The (x + 4) cancels, leaving −1/(x + 5).

Removable at x = −4 (hole), infinite at x = −5 (vertical asymptote).

Check yourself: why is f(x) = −x − 1x² + 2x + 2 continuous everywhere?  (#9)

Try to solve x² + 2x + 2 = 0. The discriminant is b² − 4ac = 4 − 8 = −4, which is negative — no real solutions. The denominator is never zero, so there are no suspects at all and the function is continuous for all real numbers.

Checking the discriminant is the move whenever a denominator won't factor.

Lesson 3

Writing the domain in interval notation

Page 18 asks for the domain every time, and it's the same information you just found — the bad x-values, removed. Sort them smallest to largest, then write the leftover pieces of the number line, joined with ∪ (union, meaning "and also").

Worked — page 18 #10

f(x) = x − 2x² − 6x + 5 = x − 2(x − 1)(x − 5). Bad values: 1 and 5, neither cancels.

Cut the number line at 1 and at 5, and keep the three surviving stretches:
(−∞, 1) ∪ (1, 5) ∪ (5, ∞)

  • Use parentheses around any excluded number and always around ∞.
  • A hole counts. In #7 the hole at x = −4 and the asymptote at x = −5 are both removed: (−∞, −5) ∪ (−5, −4) ∪ (−4, ∞).
  • A jump costs you nothing. In #8 both pieces are lines, and every real number still gets exactly one output, so the domain is (−∞, ∞) even though the function is discontinuous at x = 3. Domain and continuity are separate questions.

Lesson 4

End behavior: what happens way out there

Continuity was a local question — one specific x. End behavior is the opposite: ignore the middle of the graph entirely and ask what the outputs do as x runs off to the far left and the far right. There are only two answers per side: the graph settles toward a height, or it runs off to ±∞.

Polynomials: only the leading term matters

Take f(x) = x⁵ − 4x³ + x + 1 at x = 100. The leading term is 10,000,000,000 while −4x³ is only −4,000,000. The highest power outgrows everything else so badly that the smaller terms stop mattering. So end behavior depends on exactly two things: the degree (even or odd) and the sign of the leading coefficient.

So #11, x⁵ − 4x³ + x + 1: degree 5 is odd, lead +1 → down on the left, up on the right. And #12, −x⁴ − 4x³ − 3x² + 1: degree 4 is even, lead −1 → both ends down.

Rational functions: compare the degrees

Now a race between the top and the bottom. Whichever grows faster wins, and the winner decides the end behavior. Compare only the degrees, then only the leading coefficients — nothing else in the polynomial affects it.

Three cases

  • Bottom-heavy (degree of denominator larger): limit is 0 on both ends. Horizontal asymptote y = 0. The denominator races away and squashes the fraction.
  • Equal degrees: limit is the ratio of the leading coefficients, on both ends. That number is the horizontal asymptote.
  • Top-heavy (degree of numerator larger): no horizontal asymptote; the function runs to ±∞. Figure out the signs by treating it like the polynomial you get from dividing the leading terms.

Why the equal-degree rule works — divide every term by the highest power of x present:

The algebra behind the shortcut

4 + x2x + 6 = 4/x + 12 + 6/x

As x gets huge, 4/x and 6/x shrink to nothing, leaving 0 + 12 + 0 = 12. That's #13: both limits are ½, and y = ½ is the horizontal asymptote. Same reasoning kills #14 — degree 2 over degree 5 leaves 0/2 = 0.

Two things students mix up

A horizontal asymptote is not a wall. A graph is allowed to cross it in the middle of the picture; the asymptote only describes the far ends. Vertical asymptotes are the ones that can never be crossed.

Equal degrees means the leading coefficients, not the constants. In #18, −3x² + 12xx² − 2x − 3, the answer is −3/1 = −3 on both ends — the 12x and the −3 downstairs are irrelevant.

Check yourself: end behavior of f(x) = x² − 163x − 9  (#17)

Degree 2 over degree 1 — top-heavy, so no horizontal asymptote and the answers are infinite. Dividing leading terms gives x²/3x = x/3, which behaves like a line with positive slope.

limx→−∞ f(x) = −∞ and limx→∞ f(x) = ∞ — matching the two arms of the graph on your page.

Lesson 5

Saying it in limit notation

Problems 17–20 want the same answers written formally. Read the notation out loud and it stops being scary:

limx→∞ f(x) = 5

"As x approaches infinity, f(x) approaches 5." The thing under the arrow is where the input is going; the thing after the equals sign is where the output ends up.

  • You need two statements for end behavior — one for −∞, one for ∞ — because the two ends can do different things.
  • Writing = ∞ does not mean the limit is a number. It's shorthand for "grows without bound." Some teachers want "does not exist (∞)"; ask which your teacher prefers.
  • Reading it off a graph (#20): put your finger on the left arm and follow it left. If it flattens along a horizontal line, that height is your limit — here −5. Then do the right arm: it climbs forever, so ∞.

Lesson 6

The Intermediate Value Theorem

Here's the whole idea in one sentence: if you walk from a low place to a high place without lifting your pencil, you had to pass through every height in between. If you were at −3 feet and later at +5 feet, at some moment you were at exactly 0 — you can't skip it.

The theorem

If f is continuous on the closed interval [a, b], and N is any number between f(a) and f(b), then there is at least one c in (a, b) with f(c) = N.

The word continuous is doing all the work. Break the graph and the guarantee evaporates.

The version your worksheet uses: hunting for zeros

Problems 21 and 22 use the special case where N = 0. If f is continuous and two outputs have opposite signs, then somewhere between those two inputs the graph crossed the x-axis — so there's a zero in between.

How to run the table problems

  1. Say why the theorem applies: "f is a polynomial, so it is continuous." Write this — it's part of the answer.
  2. Evaluate f at each integer in the given range and build the table.
  3. Scan the bottom row for a sign change between neighbors.
  4. Report each one as an interval: "a zero between x = 4 and x = 5."
Worked — page 19 #21

f(x) = x² − 5x + 3 on 0 ≤ x ≤ 6. Polynomial → continuous, so IVT applies.

The sign flips twice: between x = 0 and x = 1, and between x = 4 and x = 5. So there is a zero in each of those intervals.

For #22, f(x) = 2x³ + 15x² − 4x − 33, no range is given — use the degree to know when to stop. A cubic has at most 3 real zeros, so once you've bracketed three sign changes (between −8 and −7, between −2 and −1, and between 1 and 2) you're finished. Start sampling integers around zero and work outward.

What IVT does and doesn't promise

It only proves existence. "There is a zero between 4 and 5" is the entire answer — you are not being asked to find it.

"At least one," not "exactly one." A sign change guarantees an odd number of crossings in there, and there could be three.

No sign change does not mean no zero. f(x) = x² is negative nowhere, yet x = 0 is a zero — the graph touches the axis and turns around. IVT can find crossings; it can never rule zeros out.

Continuity is mandatory. f(x) = 1/x goes from −1 at x = −1 to 1 at x = 1 but never equals 0 — the asymptote at x = 0 breaks the interval, so the theorem simply doesn't apply.

Check yourself: does f(x) = x³ − x − 1 have a zero between x = 1 and x = 2?

f(1) = 1 − 1 − 1 = −1 and f(2) = 8 − 2 − 1 = 5. It's a polynomial, so it is continuous on [1, 2], and the outputs change sign from negative to positive.

By the IVT, yes — there is at least one c in (1, 2) with f(c) = 0.
